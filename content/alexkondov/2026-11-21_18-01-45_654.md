# Why we haven't automated quality

August 24, 2026

![Bruegel, The Blind Leading the Blind (1568)](/_astro/header.BgLK6BH2_Z1Jqejn.jpeg)

Coding agents are very good. No, they’re incredible.

But many of the arguments we had against them still stand.

Their software design is questionable and often optimizes for local maxima. They overcomment and write verbosely. They produce visual design focused on looking _“designed”_ but struggle with usability.

But guess what?

The world is still spinning.

Yeah, it turns out that _“good-enough”_ is good enough for most teams out there. You can say that people want good software, perfect software, exceptional software, and some do.

But a lot of people order from Temu, so you need to know your audience.

Great will always require a personal touch and attention, but this is not about making great software or even good software. We’re concerned with why we still haven’t created an assembly line for _“good-enough”_ software.

My running theory is that this is due to the constantly changing context required for baseline quality.

## Second-order effects

Every change we make to a piece of software has obvious effects and secondary ones that are not as easy to predict.

When I worked at media, we redesigned a website and the natural result was better readability, accessibility, load times, and a more modern look.

The second-order effect _(one we didn’t foresee)_ was a broken print format, which turned out to be important for some of our aging users who actually print the web page on paper before reading it.

The natural effect of coding agents is that people push more code.

The second-order effect is that GitHub’s uptime suffers because its load has increased tenfold. The third-order effect of this is that your own development workflow gets disturbed because of GitHub actions.

The expected effect of adding slick animations to buttons is to make the UI better.

The reality is that animations on frequently-used actions make it clunkier and harder to use. They create a delay before every action which does more harm than good. If you’re on a Mac machine, open the right-click contextual menu anywhere. You’ll notice that it appears instantly, but when you click outside it fades away. Its appearance is not animated precisely because of that.

* * *

This happens both with small-scale changes in a single product and large-scale industry shifts, and a big part of maintaining the quality of a software product is foreseeing or reacting to these second- and third-order effects.

Even _“good-enough”_ software with no ambition of entering the software book of greatness needs to take care of these, and that’s what prevents it from fully turning into an assembly line.

There are small yet critical deviations from the base path.

Which is a context problem.

For a coding agent to consider some of these second-order effects, it needs broader context - about the company, about the users, about the world.

> _So why don’t we pass the context to the agent?_

The relevant context changes as the software changes. There are different parts of the company and the outside world that are relevant to every new feature.

> _So why don’t we pass all of it?_

Then the signal-to-noise ratio gets out of balance, and the agent can start over-engineering irrelevant bits of the codebase, which you once again need to review.

It’s very difficult to figure out why one embedding scored over another, for example, so the current generation of agents relies on search tools, but sometimes these second-order effects are not directly corelated and they’re not easy to spot.

I imagine that the companies working on context solutions are trying to solve this.

> _But we can have another agent review the PR of the first one based on specific rules!_

Show me a setup that’s given you good actionable results and I’ll agree.

* * *

I think that quality can’t be automated.

Some of it can, of course.

It’s naive to think that quality doesn’t benefit from automation. We’ve been using tests, linters, and various static analysis tools to guarantee a quality baseline, but this is a part of QA, not the whole. The industrialist dream is to scale up this effort in a way that allows us to produce software en masse, and to my surprise we’re close to that.

The industry has become more capital intensive than before.

But throwing money at an agent doesn’t produce the same results like throwing money at new agricultural equipment would because the second-order effect management _(still)_ requires manual effort.

And most importantly - it requires direction.
