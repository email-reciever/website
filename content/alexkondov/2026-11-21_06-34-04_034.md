# Being a stranger in my own codebase

September 22, 2026

!["Children Playing under a Gloucester Wharf"](/_astro/header.hjVqS0lm_21idUj.jpeg)

I was a lucky kid growing up.

I had these carefree summers where we’d spend all day outside, playing hide and seek, causing trouble, going home to eat, then playing some more hide and seek until it got dark and I went home only to sleep and repeat it again the next day.

I knew my neighborhood like the palm of my hand - the same way I used to know the codebases I was working on up until recently.

I knew the code by heart. The order of functions in the file, which files were in which folders, and what utility functions we had in the project. When someone told me about a problem, I could visually imagine the file where I’d start debugging and where I’d put breakpoints and log statements.

Even if I stepped away from a project for six months, when I went back, it would welcome me like an old friend.

> _Ah, yes. This is where I did that ugly hack…_

I knew other people’s hacks because I had to review them.

I could tell which of my colleagues wrote certain parts of the codebase.

I knew where each linter ignore comment lived.

But this intimate knowledge of a codebase is no longer as useful as it was.

It’s a memorization skill, and these are not as valuable when you have a tool that can walk you through a codebase without prep. I no longer need to know all the details by heart, but this has been an instrumental part of my inner reward system for more than a decade, and I’d lie if I said I don’t miss it.

When I had to make a change in someone else’s codebase, it felt like visiting another town. I’d only be there for a while, then I’d go back to what I know. But I’ve been living off a suitcase for a year now, and I don’t think I’m going to my neighborhood any time soon.

> _Only Claude writes code in this module_
> 
> an actual sentence a colleague told me

It’s the thinking machine’s project, and I’m just here to lend a hand.

To those starting out, this will be a normal feeling.

Intimate knowledge of the inner workings of a codebase was an economic necessity and a maintainability requirement, and now - a thing of the past.

The quality of the software I made depended on how well I knew the codebase. I had to anticipate how abstractions would need to change, how data models could evolve. We had to keep it human-readable because there’s an inverse correlation between understanding and the number of production incidents.

Nostalgia aside, that’s why I find it difficult to detach from the code and settle for surface-level understanding in some areas of it.

Quality depended on understanding and knowing the _“how”_.

Now quality depends more on knowing the outcome you expect and the _“what”_.

I’m still adjusting to this.
