Lately, I’ve been thinking and writing a lot about what AI is doing to product development, and what that means for accessibility. The thing that has my wheels turning is a finding from Level Access’s [2026 State of Digital Accessibility Report](https://www.levelaccess.com/sodar26/?utm_medium=paidmedia&utm_source=smashing-magazine&utm_campaign=fy26q3-ic-sodar). Basically: AI is helping us hit fast-forward on the build, but testing practices haven’t kept up.

We asked more than 2,500 professionals where they noticed AI was speeding up their work in the SDLC. Most said they noticed gains in planning (58%), design (61%), and development (51%). But QA and testing lagged behind: **Only 30%** said AI had sped up that stage.

These results point to a **two-speed workflow**. AI has turbocharged design, planning, and development, while checking and validation are still running at a very different pace. This means a lot of teams are likely racing through the build, then being forced to hit the brakes the moment someone has to answer a pretty fundamental question: Is this thing actually accessible?

Accessibility has historically been treated as a QA bottleneck, and AI is pushing far more work into it, faster. If your team has already shifted left on accessibility, you’re off to a good start. But AI has reshuffled the workflow, so even proactive teams need to work out _where_ those early checks belong now.

This matters because right now, the web is moving in the wrong direction. [WebAIM’s latest scan](https://webaim.org/projects/million/) of the top million home pages found that automatically detected errors per home page rose **10.1%** in a year, from 51 to 56.1 per page. WebAIM cited AI-assisted coding as one possible contributing factor.

So how do we get validation out of first gear? How do we build human judgment into the workflow as AI keeps pushing the pace?

AI Isn’t The Biggest Differentiator

We looked at the organizations that are improving across every accessibility metric they track (a group we call the “improvers”) to find out what they’re doing differently.

Here’s what stood out: AI adoption made the smallest difference of anything we measured. Improvers use AI at a similar rate to everyone else we surveyed. Their edge shows up in the maturity of their surrounding accessibility practices — things like **training**, **communication**, and a **culture of accessibility**.

**A quick caveat**: _This is survey data, so it only shows correlation, and mature programs may simply be better at tracking and reporting their wins. Even so, this finding was hard to ignore._

And it got even more interesting. Professionals in mature accessibility programs were nearly six times as likely to say AI helps them prioritize issues. They were also nearly three times as likely to say it helps them tackle accessibility more proactively in development.

In other words, AI works best as an amplifier of a strong program. With context, testing, environments, and guardrails in place, agents can take on a large share of the work from drafting through to operations, and much of the time lost to waiting and handoffs goes away. People stay responsible for deciding what to build and for approving what ships.

So let me ask you: When it comes to accessibility, where does human judgment sit in _your_ workflow today?

Not sure? Here’s a quick way to find out.

Pick a feature you shipped recently and trace it from the first Figma frame to production. Mark every moment a person decided whether it would work for someone using a keyboard, a screen reader, or their browser at 400% zoom.

How many marks did you get? If most of them cluster at the end of creation, you’re likely heading to one of two places: You’ll either ship accessibility barriers or spend the time you saved upstream fixing them later, when they’re harder and costlier to fix.

Here’s how to avoid both outcomes by building the right accessibility checks and judgment calls into the work your team is already doing.

Build The Judgment Calls Into Your Workflow

As AI takes on more work, people need to stay involved at three points: finding issues, fixing them, and documenting the evidence. Each one plugs into a step your team already takes, like a handoff, a pull request, or a review, so nothing new gets added to the timeline, and accessibility keeps pace with the work that’s in motion.

### 1\. Find: Catch Issues While The Work Is Still Warm

Context fades fast. Put the first judgment checkpoints as close to the creation work as you can:

*   **Start at the handoff.**  
    Spell out focus order, accessible names, states, heading structure, and what gets announced when something changes or goes wrong. If you leave anything vague, the model fills the gap with its own defaults. Then, put it where the model reads it: your design system components, rules files (Cursor rules, AGENTS.md, Copilot instructions), and prompt templates, not only a spec document. For designers, free annotation kits make this concrete. [GitHub’s Annotation Toolkit](https://primer.style/accessibility/annotation-toolkit/) offers Figma components for marking headings, landmarks, focus order, and keyboard behaviour on the canvas, so the intent travels with the file instead of living in someone’s head.
*   **Gate the pull request.**  
    Run checks in your CI/CD pipeline and at the pull request. Let them block or warn on a merge. Compare against a baseline so you only review changes. (This is the automated half of the gate: The human half of this same checkpoint shows up again under Fix). Most teams already have the tooling in place for this, but what is new is volume. AI-assisted pull requests arrive faster than reviewers can read them, so the automated gate matters more, not less. In React projects, [eslint-plugin-jsx-a11y](https://github.com/jsx-eslint/eslint-plugin-jsx-a11y) flags issues before commit, while [Pa11y](https://github.com/pa11y) and [Lighthouse CI](https://github.com/GoogleChrome/lighthouse-ci) scan builds for new errors. [Level CI](https://www.levelaccess.com/level-ci/?utm_medium=paidmedia&utm_source=smashing-magazine&utm_campaign=fy26q3-ic-sodar&utm_content=andrew-chung-article) also brings accessibility testing into the CI/CD workflow, including pre-merge detection, delta findings, quality gates, and fix recommendations in pull requests.
*   **Let automation do the sweep, send the judgment calls to people.**  
    Run automated checks across pages, screens, and documents for broad, repeatable coverage. For the sweep, [QualWeb](https://github.com/qualweb/qualweb), for example, checks pages against the W3C’s ACT rules. Running your tests across several engines, like axe-core and Equal Access, can be a good idea, as one may help you catch things that the other misses. But, have people assess usability: A dropdown can pass automated checks while its submenu remains inaccessible to keyboard users.
*   **Give every check an owner.**  
    For example, designers specify focus, errors, and states. Developers run a keyboard pass and a screen reader smoke test on custom components. (**Note:** Screen reader testing is harder in practice than it may seem at first, so your team might need an introductory training). UX or QA navigates the full journey. Put accessibility metrics in acceptance criteria and the definition of done, and back it with component-level tests because “everyone owns it” has a funny way of turning into “nobody did it.” When teams decide, in advance, who is responsible for each check, they create the clarity and communication that our data shows sets the organizations improving accessibility apart from those stuck fighting ever-growing backlogs.

### 2\. Fix: Act Before The Context Goes Cold

Found the issue? Great. Fix it while it’s fresh. When issues end up as tickets, make sure the context travels with them. They should carry the element, the repro, the related WCAG criterion, a suggested fix, and the PR link, so context doesn’t leak.

*   **Move the guidance to the work.**  
    Whether the fix happens in the editor, the design file, or the component library, the answers should be right there. If your team uses an AI coding assistant, an MCP server can pull accessibility findings and fix guidance straight into it so developers can work through issues without leaving their editor. Level Access offers one that connects to tools like Cursor and Claude.
*   **Let AI take the first pass.**  
    Use AI to draft, with guardrails. AI coding assistants can propose changes quickly, but they optimize for a passing check, not a correct experience. Give them grounded fix guidance for the specific issue, and treat their output as a draft. Open-source skill sets can help ground that guidance. A good starting point is the [accessibility instructions file](https://github.com/github/awesome-copilot/blob/main/instructions/a11y.instructions.md) in the Awesome Copilot collection, which steers Copilot toward accessible patterns. Once you’ve got your draft, re-run the check, then review the semantics and test the behaviour because a green result can come from markup that looks right and isn’t (e.g., `role="button"` on a `div`, redundant ARIA, invented alt text). The speed is useful only if verification keeps pace.
*   **Review before anything merges.**  
    Test the change in context. Did it solve the original problem without breaking anything else? Does the interaction now behave and announce itself as intended? Note: This gate is doing different work than the per-check testing upstream in “Find”: That caught the issue; this work confirms the fix actually holds in context.
*   **Work the backlog by impact.**  
    Pulling older findings? Start with things that block someone from completing a key journey, like paying a bill or resetting a password. Then tackle the quickest fixes first. Include the component, what went wrong, and the guidance to fix it. Don’t expect someone to reconstruct the story.

### 3\. Prove: Leave A Receipt

The worst time to start gathering evidence is when somebody’s asking for it.

Every check, fix, and judgment call throughout the process described above should ideally leave a trail. A fix isn’t done until something fails if the issue comes back. Ask your AI assistant to write the regression test with the fix and put the finding link and any judgment call in the PR description. That’s the receipt.

A passing test shows a known issue hasn’t returned. It doesn’t show the experience works. For what automation can’t cover, add a short note to the PR: what you tested, with which assistive technology, and any judgment calls you made.

*   **Let tooling do the admin.**  
    When someone later asks what was tested and who approved it, tooling can assemble the answer from those PRs and tests. We cut VPAT/ACR authoring time by more than 70% this way with the Level Access Reporting Agent, but only because the evidence already existed. Again, the tool compounds a practice. It doesn’t replace one.

Keep The Speed: Move The Judgment With It

AI has given digital creation a speed boost, but broadly speaking, validating the work hasn’t kept pace. Closing that gap isn’t about adopting more AI in more places or abandoning the toolset that helped make your team faster.

According to our data, the opportunity in front of teams is to build the practice around that toolset, so that **human judgment is flagged, embedded, and acted on earlier, and more often**.
