For years, enterprises have approached AI as a series of discrete experiments. Now, as AI agents begin to move into the workflows that run the business, that model is starting to look inadequate. Organizations moving from isolated pilots toward AI agents and autonomous workflows are confronting a deeper challenge: rethinking how data, business processes, governance, and people fit together, turning AI from a collection of tools into part of the enterprise operating model.

For Thorsten Leiduck, SVP and global head of SAP Business AI Platform Ecosystem, that shift begins with treating AI as an enterprise platform rather than a collection of disconnected projects. “AI is absolutely dependent on the data foundation,” he says, arguing that companies need a connected, semantically rich layer that reflects their business before they can reliably deploy AI across processes. The stakes also change as AI moves into mission-critical workflows. For some everyday applications, “80% might be good enough,” he says, but in areas such as supply chains or financial close, that level of accuracy is “a very terrible result.”

That foundation also determines which AI applications are worth pursuing. Satinder Pal Singh, associate VP North America for SAP BAIP and SAP AI Strategy at Infosys, frames AI agents as “digital coworkers” and recommends evaluating potential use cases through three lenses: impact, feasibility, and scalability. Companies should identify where an agent can move meaningful business metrics, determine whether their data and talent can support the use case, and then establish whether successful applications can be replicated. “A pilot that can’t scale is just an experiment,” he says.

Scaling also brings governance into sharper focus. Clear policies, access controls, monitoring, and oversight are necessary to ensure agents remain trustworthy and aligned with business requirements. “The absence of governance doesn’t speed up AI adoption,” Singh says. “It creates a risk backlog” that can ultimately force a more disruptive stop and audit.

Those considerations will become more important as AI agents take on more responsibilities as digital coworkers. Both executives see a future in which agentic systems coordinate work across applications, while customer-specific knowledge graphs and governed data become critical enterprise assets. For Singh, the longer-term opportunity is a shift from AI as a productivity tool to “AI as a decision-making partner,” helping leaders understand what is happening, anticipate what comes next, and determine how to respond.
